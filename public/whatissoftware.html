What is software and the problem with immortality

07/2026
(a collection of thoughts I used to examine my assumptions about software and how it compares to physical goods)

(initial observations)
Lately I have been buying a lot of physical tools, mostly vintage and of amazingly high quality in comparison to what is available on the market today. At the same time I'm starting to get a little worried about how software is being perceived, mainly on social media, which leads to doubt about softwares trajectory and a rather contrasting image compared to the ever-lasting tools. Therefore it is adequate to take some time and think these things through, as I think part of this is not founded on sincerity.
Unlike physical goods modern software can change its form significantly from the state you purchased it in. This is a counter-intuitive phenomenon given the static nature of code itself so this needs to be examined. People are changing prices post-contractually, removing features, sometimes removing the purchased product alltogether, adding unskippable ads to your medium and so on. Unlike a saw or a vise, software usually cannot be used for a persons lifetime and I'm not convinced it has to be this way.
Sure, physical goods may break down before their useful life is over, however this has not been the standard for all of mankind. A Singer brand sewing machine from 1950 may work just as well now, as it did on the first day it was purchased 76 years ago if the instructed care was provided to the machine.

(physical vs virtual)
To conclude whether software is comparable to physical products, we need to first establish what a physical product is, what its actual purpose is and then do the same for software.
A physical product is a tool to interact with the physical world. Physical products serve humans to fulfill a task: to split wood, traverse space or shelter us from the rain. They allow humans to shape the world to their needs and increase their abilities to accomplish the tasks they need to get done.
A virtual product is a product, expressed through information which interacts with the digital world, executes on a computer and achieves tasks unfit for the physical world. An unthinkable task for example is to perform very fast search in a large physical database (sure, divide and conquer but there is a limit). Try finding a book in a library as quickly as you can perform a search algorithm on a computer. (The travel time to and from the library counts as well.) It follows that the purpose of software is to implement new digital solutions to physical problems if we are comparing it strictly to tools. These solutions can be general or specific. They might be a text editor or an e-mail program to interface with a network protocol and lay out data for humans to interact with. Some programs re-implement basic features and in the same way an email program re-implements a text editor, a roofer's hammer and an engineer's hammer both implement a metalhead with a flat face on a stick. Data sheet programs such as Excel for example can be re-used endlessly and edited without degradation, which is not something we can do physically. Media such as games, working on intellectual problems and also solutions to software issues themselves are being left out in this broad characterization.

(problems with pricing perfection)
One attribute that is unique to software is the ability for it to never break or develop signs of use. This is also part of what makes software pricing difficult and why some companies appear to make software that is incomplete and broken, in need of repair. Software can be copied and perfectly replicated one to one on another system. You bought one drill bit but you can replicate it as often as you like and do so forever. That's a good product but a bad one to price to humans. The reasonable and over a lifetime technically correct price may be too high to sell to someone who isn't thinking ten steps ahead. Since you can copy it at no cost, one person buys it and supplies everyone with free copies after you crafted it meticulously to perfection, costing you many hours of your life. To a certain extent this model of pricing for the lifetime of the product has been tried with early physical media by employing copyright protection and distributing activation keys to remedy this effect. The pricing issue is not an issue of the individual but a side effect of a societal model that is incompatible with perfection as is possible in software it seems. We are organized to re-purchase consumables and commodities every month or years rather than buying a lifetime asset. This buying pattern also doesn't seems unreasonable especially considering the early development of Computers in the 90s, where after as little as 6 month the state of the art computer was superseeded by a way faster machine.

(physical things can be inherited but what about software?)
Software is immortal but the state of our world is not. There are parts of software like open standards, programming languages and typography that can be inherited and live on. Basic programs like Vi, Tar and software in the Linux ecosystem that is correctly written with foresight might still be functional today thanks to C's backward compatibility. If a C compiler exists and all the dependencies are included in the program the old software may run, assuming the systems API's are still the same. This also depends on hardware manufacturers including the old instruction set in future iterations, which is also part of the reason why we still don't have a good standard for graphics comparable to x86 ISAs. The software may not run if a programmer used undefined behavior and trickery to achieve certain functionality like using buffer overflows with 32Bit numbers or using 4bit Integers as address pointers on a 32Bit system. Old errors may also be detected by new compilers such that the compiler won't compile the code or will at least throw a warning. Software and hardware that emulate the old system in its entirety are also be available, extending systems lifetimes. A special case for longevity are closed systems: hardware + software combinations without third party authentication, which can live on for decades, see the GameBoy ecosystem or older consoles in general. Hand calculators, old CNC machines, firmware and offline, purpose built software falls in that category as well.

(friend vs foe and good design)
Anyone who helps us in achieving our goals we call friend and anyone who actively and definitively defers our ability in doing so we should avoid. However we seem to not hold our physical and virtual goods to that same standard even though small inconveniences on a daily basis compound. While any tool is better than no tool, good design serves the human in one task mainly: achieving their goals as effortlessly as possible. This requires pruning and shaping the product until no further cutting out or adding to is necessary or reasonable. The point of sufficiency. That Singer 401G has reached sufficiency for the target audience in a world that didn't change in 75 years. Sure nylon and synthetics were added but sufficiency then meant sewing thick leather and heavy fabric and by accident this extends to todays requirements as well. If this is achieved, an emotional bond to the product will develop and we may find beauty in our creation because we value things that are built with foresight and care for our human constraints (ergonomics of human shape and procedures). There are no absolutes in good design, just sufficiency. The Singer 401A is as beloved as the 401G even though they follow different shapes and outward appearances, one looking more industrial while the other looks more akin to what a person in 1950 would imagine a spaceship to look like. I am not sure there is an antidote to the difficulty of pricing perfection, but I do know that we need to be cautious of bad design, especially if it is actively keeping us from achieving our goals. It is in the hands of the consumer to make these choices but the developers produce the available choices.

(a finite reality means finite resources)
What is exciting is being first to market, solving a novel problem or extending the existing software to make it better. This seems to be the general strategy today: Product market fit -> get the paying customers -> improve the product afterwards As with all investments, timing is more important than being right. Producing software is a time investment therefore it is implicit that the above laid out strategy is actually the correct one. Yet I believe it is the duty of any developer to produce the software to sufficiency and design the system with the specific intent at hand while keeping the useful life in mind. Different rules apply to different types of software such as prototypes and educational/recreational programming of course, even though I would assert that thinking about sufficiency solves for this as well.


Software development will stay a worthwhile time investment as long as there are problems unsolvable in the physical world.
